# What the Hive Brought Home

*Fifteen hives, weighed every five minutes, revealed different daily weight patterns before, during and after bloom.*

What the Hive Brought Home. One bee leaves the hive, finds a flower, and comes home carrying a little nectar. Another makes the same trip. Across thousands of small journeys, the hive’s weight rises and falls through the day. Can that pattern tell us which part of the flowering season a colony is in?

That’s the question behind “Determining blooming-phase dynamics through intra-day hive-weight analysis.” The study, by José Luis Ávila-Jiménez and colleagues, appears in Smart Agricultural Technology. Researchers followed fifteen hives at the University of Córdoba’s experimental apiary across two consecutive beekeeping seasons. Six hives were monitored in 2016 and nine in 2017.

A remote system recorded each hive’s weight every five minutes. The team then compared the changes within each day across three phases: before flowering, during flowering, and after flowering. They also related daily weight patterns to environmental conditions and bee activity.

The clearest difference appeared in the size of the daily weight swing. During the bloom phase, the average daily amplitude was about 0.76 kilograms. Before bloom, it was about 0.47 kilograms. After bloom, it was about 0.50 kilograms. The timing of the day’s maximum weight also differed between these phases.

Comparison: think of the hive’s weight record like a traffic map. The number of cars on a road, and the times when traffic peaks, can reveal something about the rhythm of a neighbourhood. Here, weight changes offer a window onto the colony’s daily coming and going, without tracking every individual bee.

The statistical tests found strong differences between the blooming phases in both the size of the daily weight change and the timing of the maximum. That supports hive weight as one useful measure for precision beekeeping. It also suggests that continuous weighing could help researchers study how colonies respond to changing environmental conditions.

But this was an exploratory study, and the evidence has a narrow base. The work used fifteen hives from one apiary, across two seasons. A weight pattern associated with bloom is not, by itself, proof of a particular bee behaviour or cause. The authors say the results need testing at more sites and in more seasons. The study’s own limitation is important: “the small sample size and single-apiary design of this exploratory study call for validation across additional sites and seasons.”

So, return to that single bee. One trip adds almost nothing to a scale. Repeated trips, pooled across a colony, can produce a daily pattern that researchers can measure. For this group of hives, that pattern was strongest during bloom, while broader testing is still needed to see how widely it holds. I'm Amara Okafor. Small choices make a colony.

## Claims

- The study monitored fifteen hives over two consecutive beekeeping seasons, with six hives in 2016 and nine in 2017.
  > Hive weights were recorded every 5 minutes over two consecutive beekeeping seasons from fifteen hives (six in 2016, nine in 2017).
- Daily hive-weight amplitude was highest during the Bloom phase.
  > Daily hive-weight amplitude differed significantly between blooming phases ... and was highest during the Bloom phase (0.76 ± 0.55 kg) compared with Post-bloom (0.50 ± 0.98 kg) and Pre-bloom (0.47 ± 0.26 kg)
- The timing of the daily maximum weight differed between blooming phases.
  > with the timing of daily maximum weight also differing significantly between phases
- The analysis related daily weight variation to environmental conditions and bee activity.
  > The analysis examined temporal intra-day weight-variation patterns associated with pre-bloom, bloom and post-bloom phases and related daily weight variation to environmental conditions and bee activity.
- The study identifies hive weight as a useful metric for precision beekeeping, while calling for further validation.
  > These results demonstrate the value of hive weight as one key metric for precision beekeeping. ... the small sample size and single-apiary design of this exploratory study call for validation across additional sites and seasons.
