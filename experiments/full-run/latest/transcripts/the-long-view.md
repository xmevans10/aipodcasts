# Nickel isotopes reveal micrometeorite accretion on the lunar farside dominated by inner Solar System materials

*Tiny impacts have left a chemical record in material returned from the Moon’s far side.*

A handful of grey lunar dust can look perfectly ordinary. Under closer inspection, it carries a record of impacts from tiny pieces of rock and metal. The familiar surface is a kind of historical document, written in chemistry.

The episode is titled “Nickel isotopes reveal micrometeorite accretion on the lunar farside dominated by inner Solar System materials”. The paper, published in Communications Earth & Environment, comes from Kewei Chen and colleagues. They studied nickel isotopes in soil, or regolith, and rock fragments collected by China’s Chang’e-6 mission from the Moon’s far side.

Their question was simple to ask, even if the answer is written in very small numbers. Where did the particles striking the lunar surface come from? And what happens to them when repeated impacts heat and partly vaporise the material?

Nickel comes in slightly different forms, called isotopes. Their proportions can shift when material evaporates or later condenses. The researchers read those shifts in the returned samples.

Most of the soil and some rock fragments had relatively heavy nickel isotope signatures, between 1.07 and 1.25 parts per thousand. The team says this fits cumulative evaporation during repeated micrometeorite bombardment. In other words, the surface may have been repeatedly heated under conditions where the vapour didn’t simply disappear, but built up around the grains.

A subset of basaltic rock fragments showed something different. Their nickel signatures were unusually light, from minus 0.95 to minus 0.48 parts per thousand. The authors interpret that as material condensing again after evaporation. Comparison: it’s a little like reading both the scorch mark and the cooled residue after a series of tiny flashes, though the lunar process is far more complex than that picture suggests.

The broader chemical signal points towards the inner Solar System as the main source of the material that has accumulated on this part of the Moon over long periods. The estimate comes with uncertainty. Two nickel measures were seven plus or minus three, and minus six plus or minus nine. Those ranges matter. They show that the source conclusion is an inference from isotope patterns, not a direct label attached to every incoming grain.

## Claims

- The researchers analysed nickel isotopic compositions in lunar regolith and lithic clasts collected by Chang’e-6.
  > Here, we analyzed the nickel isotopic compositions of regolith and lithic clasts collected by the Chang’e-6 (CE6) mission.
- The regolith and some lithic clasts had enriched heavy nickel isotopic compositions consistent with cumulative evaporation during repeated micrometeorite impacts.
  > Our results show that CE6 regolith and some lithic clasts exhibit systematically enriched heavy nickel isotopic compositions (δ 60 Ni = 1.07‰ to 1.25‰), consistent with cumulative evaporation during repeated micrometeoritic bombardment under quasi-saturated conditions.
- Some basaltic clasts had unusually light nickel isotopic compositions, which the authors associate with recondensation after evaporation.
  > a subset of CE6 basaltic clasts records unusually light nickel isotopic compositions (δ 60 Ni = −0.95‰ to −0.48‰), beyond the range previously reported for lunar materials and indicative of post-evaporation recondensation.
- The nickel isotope anomalies suggest that long-term accreting material came mainly from the inner Solar System.
  > the nickel isotopic anomalies of the CE6 regolith (μ 60 Ni = 7 ± 3 and μ 62 Ni = −6 ± 9) suggest that the long-term average accreting materials to the lunar surface were dominantly sourced from the inner Solar System.
