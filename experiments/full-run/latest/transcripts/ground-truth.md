# Cohesin reshapes replication fork contacts to aid fork slowing and reversal

*A study in Nature finds that cohesin helps reorganize stressed DNA replication forks, supporting slowing and reversal rather than letting damage spread unchecked.*

**Ines Marlowe:** A single stalled replication fork can leave behind single-stranded DNA. How was that measured, and what happens next? The episode is titled “Cohesin reshapes replication fork contacts to aid fork slowing and reversal.”

**Dev Raman:** It matters beyond the lab because cancer treatments can challenge these copying machines, and cells need ways to keep a damaged copying process from becoming a broken genome. In Nature, Daniel González‐Acosta and colleagues report the paper “Cohesin reshapes replication fork contacts to aid fork slowing and reversal.”

**Ines Marlowe:** The researchers asked whether nearby replication forks communicate when copying is under stress, and whether cohesin helps organize that contact. Cohesin is a protein complex that helps arrange DNA in loops, rather like a movable clasp shaping a long strand.

**Dev Raman:** They combined several ways to remove or alter cohesin with a new method for capturing contacts near newly copied DNA. They found that cohesin gathers at stalled forks and changes which forks contact one another. Comparison: it’s a little like redirecting traffic around a blocked city street, so vehicles use safer connecting roads instead of crowding the jam.

**Ines Marlowe:** More specifically, loop-forming cohesin reduced direct coupling between sister forks and favoured contacts between separate replication regions. The result was linked to active fork slowing and reversal, while preventing a protein called PRIMPOL from acting on exposed single-stranded DNA.

**Dev Raman:** So the proposed picture is coordinated rather than local. A replication-stress response may involve the three-dimensional layout of the genome, not just a pile of separate repairs at individual forks. That gives cohesin a role in managing the shape of the problem as copying continues.

## Claims

- Cancer chemotherapeutic treatments can challenge DNA replication forks, causing single-stranded DNA to accumulate and DNA synthesis to slow.
  > DNA replication forks can be challenged by cancer chemotherapeutic treatments, leading to accumulation of single-stranded DNA and slowdown of DNA synthesis.
- The study examined the role of cohesin-mediated loop extrusion in replication-fork contacts under stress.
  > Although transient interaction of sister forks was recently reported during unperturbed replication 5 , the functional relevance of fork contacts during replication stress and the role of cohesin in this context remain unknown.
- The researchers used multiple experimental approaches, including a technique for capturing chromatin contacts at newly copied DNA.
  > Using auxin-inducible degron 6 , separation-of-function mutants 7–9 and a newly developed Micro-C-based technique to capture chromatin contacts at nascent DNA (Repli-C)
- Cohesin accumulates at stalled forks and favours inter-replicon contacts over sister-fork coupling.
  > we found that loop-extruding cohesin accumulates at stalled replication forks, limiting sister-fork coupling in favour of inter-replicon contacts.
- The process promotes fork slowing and reversal by preventing PRIMPOL action on single-stranded DNA.
  > This process promotes active fork slowing and reversal by preventing PRIMPOL action on single-stranded DNA 1 .
- The results suggest that replication-stress responses are organized across the genome through cohesin loop extrusion.
  > These findings show that the replication stress response is not merely an accumulation of individual regulatory events, but is topologically integrated across the genome through cohesin loop extrusion.
