**Title:** Growth-rate coordination across the width of a leaf preserves its flatness

**Dek:** A tiny mustard relative reveals that keeping a leaf flat depends on coordination across its width—not simply growth from tip to base.

Watch a young leaf unfurl under the microscope and it looks less like a green flag and more like a very busy construction site. Cells expand, divide and mature in place, without neighbours stepping aside. So how does the whole thing avoid becoming a crumpled crisp?

**Growth-rate coordination across the width of a leaf preserves its flatness**. That’s the title of a study by Kate Harline and colleagues, published in *PLOS Biology*. They examined *Arabidopsis thaliana*, the small mustard relative that has become something of a botanical laboratory regular.

The researchers compared ordinary plants with a mutant called *jagged and wavy*, or jaw-D. Its leaves develop ripples and won’t stay flat. The team repeatedly imaged the same young leaves from three to eight days after sowing, using confocal microscopy to track cells across nearly the entire lower surface. There were just three leaves of each genotype in this live-imaging experiment, so this is detailed work at small scale, not a census of every possible leaf.

The striking result was that the mutant’s problem wasn’t simply “too much growth”. In normal leaves, growth along the length changes from the base towards the tip, but at each height it’s relatively even across the width. In jaw-D leaves, the midrib—the central region over the main vein—grew more slowly than the surrounding blade. That mismatch created mechanical conflicts, and the leaf curved across its whole surface rather than merely frilling at the edge.

Comparison: imagine stretching a sheet of pastry while the middle and sides move at different speeds. The sheet doesn’t politely remain flat; it buckles. The modelling here produced a similar outcome. When growth was coordinated across the width, the simulated leaf stayed flat. When it wasn’t, curvature appeared. Strongly uneven growth could even generate a saddle shape reminiscent of a potato crisp—nature’s geometry, with fewer snacks.

The team also found that jaw-D leaves had a delayed pattern of maturation and a less clearly defined growth gradient from base to tip. Yet mature mutant and normal leaves had similar median sizes. That’s a useful reminder: final size alone doesn’t reveal how an organ got its shape. The simulations support a mechanism, but they are models based on manually specified growth rates, not direct measurements of mechanical forces in every cell.

This study used early leaves of one *Arabidopsis* genotype and mutant, with three leaves per genotype for the live-imaging series. Some curved jaw-D regions could not be segmented, proximal cells were likely undercounted, and cover slips and agar constrained the real leaves during imaging. The finite-element simulations used simplified material assumptions and growth rates set from qualitative imaging assessment. Gaps caused by trichomes also prevented a strong conclusion about coordination on the upper leaf surface, and the findings may not apply unchanged to other species with different growth patterns.
