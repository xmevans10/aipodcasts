# Effects of surface defects on flow and heat transfer in additively manufactured ellipsoidal Kelvin lattices

*A printed lattice can look perfect in a computer model, while roughness and tiny shape errors change how air moves through the real part.*

An ellipsoidal Kelvin lattice can look like a piece of impossible metal lace: curved struts, narrow passages, and a shape that seems designed to make a caliper give up. Build it one layer at a time, though, and the real object brings along rough skin and small shape errors. Those details can change the airflow and the heat it carries away.

That’s the subject of Effects of surface defects on flow and heat transfer in additively manufactured ellipsoidal Kelvin lattices. Yu Liu and colleagues report the work in International Communications in Heat and Mass Transfer.

The researchers printed six versions of these lattices from AlSi10Mg, an aluminium alloy. The struts ranged from half a millimetre to one millimetre thick. They measured the printed shapes, including their roughness and deviations from the clean digital design. Then they put those reconstructed shapes into computer flow simulations and checked the predictions with wind-tunnel experiments.

The test piece was fifty by fifty by ten millimetres. Air entered at speeds from five to twenty metres per second, while the lattice was heated with a steady heat input. The experiments and simulations matched well for pressure drop and heat transfer, which gives the comparison some useful footing.

Now for the workshop trade-off. With the thinnest struts, roughness helped stir the air near the metal surface. Comparison: it’s a little like adding texture to a paint roller, giving the moving air more chances to mix instead of sliding smoothly past. At the highest tested speed, the rough printed surface raised the heat-transfer coefficient by fourteen percent compared with the ideal model. The bill arrived as extra pressure loss, so the fan had to work harder.

With the thickest struts, the balance flipped. Geometric deviations became more important than roughness, and the heat-transfer coefficient fell by fourteen point nine percent compared with the ideal model. A thicker strut wasn’t automatically a better heat exchanger. The digital model had one answer. The printed cross-section had another.

There are limits to what this tells us. The study covered six AlSi10Mg lattice configurations, one lattice size, a constant heat input, and inlet speeds from five to twenty metres per second. The results were checked with wind-tunnel tests, but they don’t establish how other materials, printing conditions, lattice sizes, or operating conditions would behave. And because the study combines measurements with simulations, the separate contribution of every surface defect isn’t measured in isolation.

So when a lattice looks flawless on screen, keep a close eye on the first printed layer. Roughness can improve mixing, while shape error can hurt it. The failure mode depends on the strut size and the flow conditions. I'm Marek Novak. Build it, then break it.

## Claims

- The researchers studied additively manufactured AlSi10Mg ellipsoidal Kelvin lattices in six unit-cell configurations with strut diameters from 0.5 to 1.0 millimetres.
  > Six unit-cell configurations with strut diameters of 0.5–1.0 mm were fabricated
- The study combined measurements of printed geometry with computer simulations and wind-tunnel validation.
  > The reconstructed geometries were used for CFD simulations and validated by wind-tunnel experiments
- For small struts, roughness increased heat transfer at the highest tested speed, while also increasing pressure loss.
  > For small struts (0.5 mm), roughness-enhanced near-wall mixing dominates, resulting in a 14% higher convective heat transfer coefficient than the ideal model at 20 m/s, although with increased pressure loss.
- For large struts, geometric deviations reduced the heat-transfer coefficient relative to the ideal model.
  > For large struts (1.0 mm), geometric deviations become dominant, reducing the heat transfer coefficient by 14.9% compared with the ideal model.
- The influence of roughness and geometric deviations depends on strut diameter.
  > Their influence depends on strut diameter.
