# Can a Painting Change Your Sense of Time?

*A study using Van Gogh paintings finds that perceived duration reflects both the details in an image and the viewer’s biological timing context.*

Can a Painting Change Your Sense of Time? Have you ever watched a second hand, blinked, and felt that the moment had stretched or snapped shut? While we sleep, the brain moves through measurable stages. While we’re awake, it also keeps estimating time from the world around us. A new study asks how much a painting can bend that estimate.

The paper is titled “When Paintings Change Time: Visual Structure and Circadian Context Shape Subjective Duration.” Cehao Yu and colleagues published it in Art & Perception. They showed participants images based on five Van Gogh paintings. There were 25 images altogether: the five originals, plus versions shifted toward four different color directions.

The task was simple in principle. Each image appeared for a brief interval. Participants judged whether that interval seemed closer to a learned short duration or a learned long one. The researchers then compared those judgments with features of the paintings, such as brightness, color spread, and the complexity of directional patterns. They also looked at each participant’s chronotype, meaning their usual timing preference, along with day length.

Color alone had a small effect. Blue-shifted images were judged as lasting slightly longer than neutral and green-shifted images. But the average judgments for those conditions stayed close to the true midpoint. The larger differences appeared between individual paintings. The shortest and longest perceived durations differed by about 22 milliseconds. That’s a tiny interval, but it was consistent enough to relate to measurable visual structure.

Three groups of image features together accounted for 59 percent of the differences between paintings. The researchers also found that chronotype and day length independently predicted perceived duration. In this sample, those observer-level effects were larger than the effects linked to the image statistics.

A comparison may help here. Think of perception as a timetable that’s adjusted by both the train and the station. The painting supplies the changing scenery, while the viewer’s internal clock and the season help set the schedule. The result isn’t a coded message hidden in the artwork. It’s a report from a brain estimating time while looking at structured visual input.

The study has limits. It used a small, specialized set of images derived from five Van Gogh paintings, so the findings may not apply to other artworks, viewing conditions, or populations. The color effects were modest, and the observational links with chronotype and day length cannot establish that circadian or seasonal factors caused changes in perceived duration. ## Claims

- The study tested perceived duration using original and hue-shifted versions of five Van Gogh paintings.
  > Stimuli comprised 25 images derived from five Van Gogh paintings: five originals and 20 hue-shifted variants created by shifting each painting in four chromatic directions.
- Blue-shifted images were judged as lasting slightly longer, but the color-condition effects were modest.
  > At the condition level, blue-shifted images were judged as lasting slightly longer than neutral and green-shifted images, as indexed by lower points of subjective equality, although these effects were modest and condition means did not differ from the objective midpoint.
- Perceived duration differed across individual paintings by about 22 milliseconds.
  > At the item level, perceived duration varied more strongly across paintings: the difference between the paintings with the shortest and longest durations was approximately 22 ms.
- Three image-statistics components explained 59 percent of painting-level variance.
  > This heterogeneity was predicted by three image-statistics components reflecting brightness and oriented texture, chromaticity–luminance dispersion, and cool structural complexity, together explaining 59% of painting-level variance.
- Chronotype and day length independently predicted perceived duration, with larger observer-level effects than image-statistics effects in this sample.
  > A participant-by-painting mixed-effects model confirmed these effects and showed that chronotype and day length independently predicted perceived duration. In this sample, the observer-level effects of chronotype and day length were larger than the image-statistics effects.
