# Where Abyssal Life Follows the Nodules

*Photographic surveys in the Northwest Pacific found that seafloor animal density dipped, then rose sharply, as polymetallic nodule cover increased.*

At around four thousand metres down, sunlight has been gone for a very long time. The seafloor is cold, dark, and scattered with potato-sized mineral lumps called nodules that animals can use as footholds. So what happens when those nodules become more common? The answer is stranger than a simple increase. This episode is called Where Abyssal Life Follows the Nodules.

The paper is titled “Pronounced megafaunal community transition driven by polymetallic nodule cover in the Northwest Pacific abyss.” Leyi Fang and colleagues published it in Communications Earth & Environment. They used a large set of seafloor photographs from a nodule-rich basin in the northwest Pacific, looking across places with different amounts of these mineral lumps. The images showed 169 animal forms from 14 broad groups. Sea cucumbers and sponges were among the dominant animals.

The central question was whether more nodules simply mean more large seafloor animals. They don’t, at least not in a straight line. Animal density fell as nodule cover increased from low to intermediate levels. Around the middle, it stayed fairly steady. Then, where nodule cover was high, density rose sharply. The result makes a shallow slope with a dip in the middle, rather than a neat upward trend.

The community changed too. At low nodule cover, mobile animals that feed on material lying on the sediment were more common. At high cover, animals that stay attached and filter food from the water became more common. The shift was driven mainly by changing numbers of the dominant animals, rather than by many species disappearing and being replaced by others.

That changes how we picture this very deep seafloor. More nodules were linked to different kinds of animals: some moved over sediment for food, while others stayed attached and filtered food from the water.

These are photographic observations from one nodule-rich basin, so they show an association between nodule cover and community patterns, not that nodules directly caused the changes. The survey also cannot tell us how well these patterns represent other abyssal regions, and image-based records provide only a partial view of animals on the seafloor.

The opening question now has a careful answer. In this Northwest Pacific basin, nodules were linked with a change in who could make a living on the bottom, with animal density dipping before rising at the highest cover. ## Claims

- The survey recorded 169 morphospecies across 14 phyla, with holothurians and sponges dominant.
  > A total of 169 morphospecies from 14 phyla were recorded, with holothurians and sponges comprising the dominant taxa.
- Megafaunal density followed a U-shaped pattern across nodule cover.
  > megafaunal density exhibited a U-shaped response to nodule cover, declining from low to intermediate nodule cover, remaining relatively stable at intermediate levels, and increasing sharply at high nodule cover.
- Community composition shifted from mobile deposit feeders to sessile filter feeders as nodule cover increased.
  > Community composition shifted markedly from mobile deposit-feeder dominance at low cover to sessile filter-feeder dominance at high cover.
- The transition was mainly caused by changes in the abundance of dominant taxa rather than species replacement.
  > This transition was primarily driven by changes in the relative density of dominant taxa rather than species turnover.
- The Northwest Pacific had lower megafaunal density and different assemblages than the Clarion–Clipperton Zone, but similar diversity.
  > Compared with the Clarion–Clipperton Zone (CCZ), the NWP showed lower megafaunal density and distinct assemblage composition but comparable diversity
